## Q: Why does Lithuania make sense as a manufacturing hub?

The Lithuania choice is deliberate and multi-layered. First, it's a NATO member — which means components manufactured there carry a different export classification status under ITAR-adjacent European frameworks than components made in Ukraine (which remains a non-NATO partner under active conflict conditions). That matters enormously for selling to, say, Poland, Estonia, or directly to a NATO pooled-procurement mechanism.

Second, Lithuania already has aerospace manufacturing depth. Companies like **FL Technics** (Vilnius) provide MRO services for commercial aviation — a talent and tooling ecosystem that a drone-engine startup can tap. ## Deep dive: How Ukrainian defense startups are building NATO-compatible hardware pipelines

The Vigilant Works story isn't isolated. It's one data point in a structural shift happening across Ukraine's defense-tech ecosystem — and understanding that shift requires looking at the broader architecture being assembled.

Since Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022, Ukraine has become, involuntarily, the world's most active live-fire testing ground for autonomous systems. That operational reality has created something no peacetime accelerator can replicate: a continuous feedback loop between battlefield deployment and product iteration. Ukrainian drone startups are shipping, failing, learning, and re-shipping on timelines that would be impossible in any other market.

The Brave1 platform, launched by Ukraine's Ministry of Digital Transformation in 2023, has been the institutional scaffolding for this. By mid-2026, Brave1 had facilitated procurement relationships for over 80 defense-tech startups — providing not just funding pathways but actual combat deployment opportunities. This is what gives companies like Vigilant Works credible field data to show Western investors. You can't fake Algiz-2 interception telemetry from a real operational theater.

But field performance alone doesn't get you a US investment round. What does it is the combination of field data *plus* a scalable manufacturing story *plus* an export-compatible supply chain. The Lithuania engine partnership is Vigilant Works answering that third requirement explicitly.

According to the **Mordor Intelligence Counter-UAS Market Report 2025**, the fastest-growing segment of the C-UAS market through 2030 is kinetic intercept systems — growing at a projected 18.4% CAGR — precisely because electronic warfare and jamming systems are increasingly being countered by adversary drone manufacturers building frequency-hopping and GPS-independent guidance. Kinetic interception, done autonomously, is the response. Algiz-2 is positioned directly in that segment.

**Dr. Zachary Kallenborn**, a UAS warfare analyst affiliated with the National Defense University (writing in *War on the Rocks*, March 2026), noted that "the next 24 months will determine which C-UAS startups achieve enough manufacturing scale to matter strategically — the technology race is largely settled; the production race has just begun." Vigilant Works' Lithuania move is a direct response to that production race.

There's also a broader geopolitical logic. Baltic states — Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania — have been among the most aggressive European nations in building defense-industrial relationships with Ukraine. Lithuania in particular signed a bilateral defense cooperation agreement with Ukraine in January 2026 that specifically included provisions for co-manufacturing of defense components. Vigilant Works' engine partnership fits neatly within that framework, likely benefiting from favorable IP and customs arrangements.

For Ukrainian defense startups watching this, the template is becoming clear: build and test in Ukraine, manufacture in a Baltic or Polish partner nation, raise capital in the US or UK, and target NATO procurement as the eventual exit or revenue ceiling. Vigilant Works isn't pioneering this path — but it's executing it more visibly than most.

**Q: Is Vigilant Works' US investment amount known?**

**Q: How does Algiz-2 differ from jamming-based counter-drone systems?**

Jamming and spoofing systems work by disrupting the communication or navigation signals a drone relies on. They're effective against commercially sourced drones but increasingly less so against military-grade UAVs with frequency-hopping radios and INS-based navigation that doesn't rely on GPS. Algiz-2, as a kinetic interceptor, physically destroys the target regardless of its electronic architecture. This makes it relevant against both commercial off-the-shelf drones and more sophisticated adversary platforms — a broader threat coverage profile.

**Q: What is the Angel One Fund and why does its prior involvement matter?**

Angel One Fund is a Ukrainian early-stage venture fund that has been active in defense and deep-tech investments since before the full-scale invasion. Its backing of Vigilant Works provided not just capital but Ukrainian market validation and likely Brave1 ecosystem access. For US investors conducting due diligence, an existing institutional Ukrainian co-investor with defense-sector credibility reduces information asymmetry — the US fund isn't flying blind into an unfamiliar market. It's a trust-and-verification signal that meaningfully lowers the perceived risk of the round.
